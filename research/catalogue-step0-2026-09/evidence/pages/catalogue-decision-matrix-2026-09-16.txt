## How tried and tested each model is

Every platform list on this page is sorted by this score, and each name carries it. The score is my proposed judgement, not evidence from the records: it estimates how much real catalogue and transaction volume has run through this exact product model, and for how long, relative to Amazon at 100. Reassign freely; the sort follows the number.

Platform | Basis for the number | 

Amazon | The largest product catalogue in the set; the type-and-parentage model has run across 20+ marketplaces for two decades and every other marketplace copied parts of it. | 

Shopify | Millions of merchant stores on one product/variant/option model since the late 2000s; the taxonomy-and-metafield layer is newer (2024) and still changing quarterly. |

🔒 Locked 2026-09-16

The category is the product type · every product belongs to a group
 One object, not two: the Category tree owns the attribute definitions and the axis eligibility, and ProductClass retires.
 The variant group is mandatory — a separate table, a NOT NULL key from the sellable row, and a family of one for the products that will never have a sibling.
 Whether the two tables end up named Product → ProductVariant or ProductGroup → Product stays open.

 Taken after four schema designs, an adversarial review, and four system passes across py/mono/solvent and ts/.
 The full record — what was rejected and why, what each lock costs, the five conditions it was taken on, and what would reopen it — is
 #10966 comment 5693781761.

 Order of work: the category attribute schema editor first (it does not exist in any form today and blocks every shape), then the four shipping dimensions out of the attribute system, then lock 2 before lock 1 — the group delivers a capability on day one, the merge is a day-one no-op.

### Layer 2 — four decisions, none waiting on another

Each is blocking: leave it open and the work downstream either stalls or gets built twice. Two are the direct bill of the two locks; two are structural questions the locks exposed.

Decision | Why it is next | Options, and what our own data says | 

 L2.1 · Does a category's attribute set inherit down the tree?D15 → D16 | 
 Everything about the registry is unsized until this is answered. Requiredness (D17), the closed contract (D18) and axis eligibility (D4) all hang off a (type, attribute) membership row that does not exist until we know where membership attaches. It also decides whether the 19,459 products sitting on non-leaf nodes are legal or a migration. | 
 (a) self-contained per node · (b) inherit down with override · (c) a universal set on the root plus leaf additions.

Our five attributes are weight, length, width, height, manufacturer — all universal. Under (a) they must be attached to each of the 1,684 nodes that hold products on day one, which is why (a) is hard to defend. Precedent for (b) is thinner than I first wrote — see the inheritance survey below. Salesforce is the only platform that inherits attribute definitions down a category tree; Akeneo does it down a family tree, which is a different object. commercetools declares the level on the definition itself, but its ProductType is flat, so nothing inherits there at all. | 

 L2.2 · Is an attribute value a shared row, or a string copied per product?D8 → D9 | 
 An axis needs values that can be enumerated, ordered and renamed. We have no option or enum attribute type — the six are text, integer, boolean, float, date, datetime — so D4 · D5 · D7 and the variant key have nothing to point at until this is settled. It is also the only part of the model with pain that is measurable today. | 
 (a) a shared value row by FK, which needs a new option attribute type · (b) a copied string with normalisation and a validator · (c) hybrid by attribute type.

Measured on manufacturer, 2026-09-15 — see the table below. Ordering, rename and normalisation are free under (a) and (c), and hand-built under (b). | 

 L2.3 · Which table keeps the name Product?the bill of lock 2 | 
 Deferred on purpose when shape B was decided, and now the thing that blocks writing any migration. It decides whether 19 relations re-point or stay put, and whether catalogue.view_product, every serializer, every admin registration and the customer URL keep their meaning. | 
 (a) Product stays the sellable row and a new ProductGroup sits above it — nothing re-points, the new table is genuinely new · (b) Product becomes the family and the sellable row becomes ProductVariant — truer to the four container platforms, where the container is the product row, but every FK, permission string and URL moves.

The tentative layer-1 decisions put price, stock, barcode, visibility and attribute values on the member, which pulls toward (a). | 

 L2.4 · One type plus a separate placement object, or many types?D14 · the bill of lock 1 | 
 Lock 1 gave the tree schema duty, so a merchandising node like "Promo Ramadan" now has nowhere to live. This was the stated cost of the lock, and it comes due as soon as anyone needs to group products for a reason that is not their schema. | 
 (a) one type plus a separate multi-valued placement object — the ProductCategory M2M, 19.6% populated and otherwise dead, is the candidate edge · (b) schema-less merchandising nodes in the same tree · (c) one Category class with role-typed edges, as Salesforce does.

Every merchant tool in the survey supports many categories per product; every marketplace is single or capped. |

 The one-line version. Layer 2 is two structural questions — do attributes inherit down the tree, and is a value a row or a string — plus two bills that the locks already incurred: which table keeps the name Product, and where merchandising nodes live. ## Item 5 — what actually flows down a type tree

 ⚠ A correction to what I wrote in the layer plan. I said self-contained-per-node was "hard to defend" and that inheritance was obviously needed. The survey says the opposite: self-contained per node is what almost everyone ships. Only Salesforce inherits attribute definitions down a category tree, and Akeneo's inheritance is on its family tree, a different object from its category tree. I had also cited Salesforce's "cannot be overridden" as tree precedent; that sentence is about the master→variant product tree, not the category tree, and on the category tree Salesforce's own word is "override". Both are fixed above.

### What this means for our three open pieces

Our question | What the survey changes | 

 L2.1 · Do attributes inherit down our tree? | 
 The industry answer is no, and the reason is worth noticing: the platforms that materialise per node either author the taxonomy centrally (Shopify, Amazon, Walmart, Shopee) or have a flat type registry that is not a tree at all (commercetools, Magento, Walmart). Neither of those describes a 1,892-node tree that our own staff author by hand. So the real choice is not "inherit or not" — it is adopt a centrally-authored taxonomy and materialise per node, or keep authoring our own tree and build inheritance to make it affordable. That ties item 5 directly to item 3. | 

 The 19,459 products on non-leaf nodes | 
 Not automatically illegal. Amazon, Shopify, Google, Magento, WooCommerce, Square, commercetools and Salesforce all permit it; eBay, Walmart, Shopee and Tokopedia forbid it. The most useful model is Akeneo's: leaf-only is a per-tree setting rather than a law, so the rule can be tightened later without a migration. | 

 If we do build inheritance, what is the reference? | 
 eBay's, which is the only fully worked one: "Each child category in the tree inherits its settings from its parent category, so the defaults set at the root node flow down through the rest of the tree… there are some categories that 'override' the root settings". It is delivered as an inheritance override model, and eBay tells integrators to "Store the data locally and then manage the data on the client side" — i.e. even the platform that inherits does not compute the effective set on every read. | 

 Route health, stated because two of these rows carry weight. Salesforce's ancestor-category walk rests on one route, a single generated doc set. Every Akeneo Family-tree fact comes from help.akeneo.com only and the feature is absent from the open-source edition. Google's "requiredness reaches descendants" also has one route. And developer.ebay.com refused all six fetch attempts with HTTP 403, so every eBay artifact here is a Wayback capture of eBay's own pages, dated 2025-01-12 to 2026-02-08.

## The three questions, highest level first

D1, D2, D3 and D13 were closed on 2026-08-13 against four platforms. The evidence beneath them is now thirteen, and the reading changed. Nothing here reopens a decision; it lays the thirteen out so each can be confirmed or re-made deliberately.

Q1
### Is the ProductType the same object as the Category?
D1 · D2 — closed 2026-08-13

Two different things get called "category": the object that decides which attributes a product has (the type) and the object a customer browses to. One row, or two?

 ✅ Locked 2026-09-09 — one object. The product type is the category; D2 stands on the thirteen-platform evidence.
 Reasons given: (1) a separate category object means re-homing every product — too much work for the gain; (2) a separate browse/merchandising object can be added later. (2) confirmed: a browse-only secondary category, collection or tag object is additive — a new object and one M2M edge, no schema touched, no product re-homed; the dead ProductCategory table is already that edge, and Shopify, eBay, Square and Salesforce all bolt browsing on this way. The only thing that is not easy later is moving schema ownership off this tree onto a new type object, which is the option rejected here and never needs to happen. What the lock makes concrete: the existing tree keeps schema duty, so every product must sit on a node whose schema it obeys — remeasured 2026-09-15, 19,459 products sit on non-leaf nodes and 1,722 on roots, a tidy-up for D15 / D16. Downstream: D14 becomes "one type plus a separate placement object when needed"; D15 and D16 are the remaining bill.

Filed 2026-09-15 → #11188 · how categories are created and managed once the category IS the product type. The lock makes every tree operation a schema migration for the products underneath it, and none of that is designed. The issue carries the starting position — one ProductClass, five attributes total (weight, length, width, height, manufacturer), so there is no per-type schema to refactor, only one to build — and the open questions: attribute inheritance down the tree, the 19,459 products on non-leaf nodes, what a move, merge, delete or reparent does to the products under it, and where schema editing lives now that attributes are Django-admin-only.

All three merges are marketplaces with a vendor-fixed taxonomy — but marketplaces are split three to three: Amazon and Walmart keep the two apart, Google has no type. #### 2a · Pros and cons of each container shape — for deciding

*Analysis written 2026-09-09, restructured 2026-09-10. > - A — the family is a parent row in the Product table; only real families have one.

> - B — the family is a separate container row, and every product belongs to one; a standalone is a container of one.

> - C — the family is a separate container row, and only real families have one; a standalone has none.

Shape A — a parent row in the same product table

For

**The sellable row stays Product. Children are ordinary products, so the till, stock records, prices, basket and order lines, promo targets, search documents and the Merchant feed keep pointing at the table they point at today. U** (inference from #11031's code-state table)
Migration is additive. Two columns, upc made nullable, one parent row per family; no existing row moves. U inference
One page per family for free. Amazon: children are "grouped together by a parent listings item to provide a single detail page experience." S SP-API workflows guide. Adobe: "A configurable product displays as a single product with dropdown options for variations (like color or size)." S
Per-child stock and price with a shared identity. Adobe: "Each variation is a separate simple product with its own SKU, enabling individual inventory tracking." S; ERPNext: template edits propagate — "when you update this template, all the variants are also updated accordingly." S
Shared content has a home with no new machinery: the parent is a product, so title, description, images and attribute values sit where they sit today. U inference
Fast reads, one table. Ambler via Stack Overflow: "Data access is fast because the data is in one table." S (2010, old)
Amazon and Magento, the two highest-scored platforms that ship it, pair it with a marked-attribute axis — which is our D3. R

Against

A non-sellable row lives where every sellable read happens. "Never sell a parent" becomes a standing invariant across the twelve sellable-only consumers the design below lists; upc (unique, non-null, checksum-validated) must become nullable, which breaks the search guid field and four other paths. U #11031, #10778
Every read path needs a rule for which rows show. Magento carries an explicit per-row visibility — 4 (Catalog, Search) on the container, 1 (Not Visible Individually) on the child — and Adobe's own guidance lists slow category pages among the costs of complex products. R #11082 §2c S Adobe. WooCommerce shows a price range on the parent in archive pages and hides variations that have no price. S
The parent's price and stock are derived, and the derivation is a known bug source. WooCommerce: parent _price "is not set correctly, leading to an invalid state" (#24947); Magento: "The configurable product is still OOS" after children restock (#35494); Adobe: "A configurable product does not have its own price in the catalog." S Our records agree: Magento emits price 0 on the container and its manual Out-of-Stock is one the children cannot undo; WooCommerce deletes and re-derives the parent price. R #11082 §2b, #11080 §2b
The role column has low selectivity, so every sellable query carries a filter. GitLab: "Don't design new tables using Single Table Inheritance"; tables that "Add overhead by having to filter all of the data by a value". DBA.SE: WHERE type='X' "is basically doing a full table scan anyway". S (a partial index on the role column answers this in PostgreSQL — inference)
The shape comes with rules about what a child may carry, and they are stricter than they look. Adobe: "Child products can be simple or virtual products without custom options."; "Variation attributes must have a global scope, and customers must be required to choose a value."; "A configurable product must always have a weight." S Amazon: a parent "must not be a buyable product", and re-parenting means deleting the relationship first — "You must remove a child listing from its current parent-child relationship before you relate it to a new parent." S We would write our own rules; these show the kind that accumulate. inference
Large families hurt. Adobe lists slow product pages, admin saves and checkout for products with many variations and advises splitting them; Magento #32703 broke Elasticsearch at 1,440 children; WooCommerce switches to static dropdowns above 30 variations. S Our families are small — Indomie is 33 live rows, grocery packs are 2–10 — so this is a ceiling we are unlikely to reach. U inference
Vendor-side oddities of the parent write path. Amazon: creating a parent has returned ACCEPTED without creating one (#2489); reading parentage_level back is inconsistent (#2701). S Amazon-specific, recorded for completeness.
Shape B — every product belongs to a family container

*Design: keep Product as the sellable row; add a ProductVariantGroup table; Product.variant_group is NOT NULL and a group is created with every product; a standalone is a group of one with no dimensions. Shared content either stays on the members or moves up to the group. The platforms that ship B (Shopify, Square, commercetools, Tokopedia Era B) make their own product row the container instead of adding one above it; their evidence is used below where it carries over.*

For

One code path. Every product is a member, so promo range filling, sibling collapse in search and the variant picker never branch on "does this product have a family?". U inference The Merchant feed is the exception — see the feed bullet under Against.
The family's type and dimensions always exist. Turning a standalone into a family is "add members and dimensions to the container it already has", with no create-and-re-home step — the argument behind the default variant: Shopify's record, "There is no 'no variant' state". R #11011 §2c

Reading (mine, not evidence)

Two questions separate the three shapes. **Is the family row a Product?** A says yes: shared content and a page come free, and the cost is a non-sellable row among sellable ones — an invariant on twelve consumers and a nullable barcode. B and C say no: Product stays purely sellable, and the cost is a second typed object. Must every product have a family? B says yes: one code path, bought with 105,256 singleton groups that leak wherever this pattern ships and that the feed must hide from Google. C says no, as every flat-row platform does; the cost is a "has a group?" branch wherever the family matters. Running across both is D11: whether shared content lives on the family row (A always; B and C optionally) or is replicated on members (the source of today's drifting Indomie rows unless tooled). The records place Amazon and Magento, the two most tried-and-tested platforms that share our axis decision, on A; B is shipped by Shopify, Square, commercetools and Tokopedia Era B, each with its product row as the container; C is shipped as a row by eBay and Akeneo. Twelve consumers are sellable-only. That list is the invariant surface for any shape that puts a non-sellable row in Product.
Common to all three designs

**The product type is the Category node** (Q1 locked: one object). It is the thing that decides what a family may vary on, in three steps that are the same in all three designs: (i) ProductAttribute.category replaces product_class — the type owns the attribute definitions (D1 + D2; whether a node also offers its ancestors' attributes is D15); (ii) the type marks which of its attributes may be an axis — ProductAttribute.is_axis_eligible (D4's gate; eBay's aspectEnabledForVariations and Akeneo's type whitelist are the precedents; the alternative is to declare no gate and let any attribute of the type be an axis, as Square and WooCommerce do); (iii) the family picks its dimensions from that eligible set (D5, the eleven-of-thirteen shape). Optionally the type can go one step further and declare named dimension sets a family must choose whole — Amazon's variation_theme — as a ProductTypeVariationTheme table; not sketched here because D5 is open.
The chain is therefore: Category (type) → owns ProductAttribute rows → some flagged axis-eligible → the family's dimension through-table may only reference those → each member stores a value for each dimension. The type constrains the family at step (ii) and (iii); it never stores the axis itself.
An option attribute type is needed before any axis exists: today's six value columns cannot hold "Ayam Bawang" as anything but free text (D8).
Dimension selection is a family-level list of ProductAttribute rows, each required to belong to the family's category and to be axis-eligible there (steps ii–iii above).
What a dimension row is. ProductVariantDimension / ProductVariantGroupDimension is a real table but a thin one: family FK, attribute FK, position. It marks the role and owns nothing else — the name is the attribute's, the allowed values are the attribute's option list, whether the list is closed or free text is the attribute's setting (and under D1 + D2 grocery Ukuran and apparel Ukuran are two attributes owned by two categories, so no per-family flag is needed), each member's value is its ProductAttributeValue row, and eligibility is the attribute's is_axis_eligible. Precedent: Magento's catalog_product_super_attribute (product id, attribute id, position, per-store label); Amazon is thinner still (a theme string on the parent); Shopify and Square made the option a full object with its own values, the D3 alternative not taken. The row can grow a display-label override or a picker default later without breaking D3.
Axis storage is the member's own ProductAttributeValue rows for the dimension attributes — D3 taken literally, one table for axis and non-axis values alike.
**variant_key**: a denormalised canonical string of the member's dimension values (for example flavour=soto;size=85g), unique per family, so D7's uniqueness is a database constraint rather than a validator over many rows. Akeneo stringifies for the same reason; Magento keys the pair.
Type equality: every member's main_category equals the family's category — a validator (or derived at save) in all three shapes; Amazon's parent is the same product type as its children, Magento's container and child share attribute_set_id.
Members keep upc exactly as today.

Rows added for a 33-flavour family | 1 parent + 33 children (existing rows become children) | 1 group + 33 members, and 1 group for every other product (105,256 in total) | 1 group + 33 members (existing rows become members) | 
upc | nullable | untouched | untouched | 
Reopens a closed decision | no (D13 as written) | yes, D13, plus a question D13 never asked | yes, D13 | 
What the exercise adds to the pros and cons

The three designs share almost everything. The type's side — Category owning ProductAttribute, the is_axis_eligible gate, the rule that a family's dimensions come from its category's eligible attributes — is identical, as are the dimension table, variant_key, the type-equality rule and the option attribute type. A differs from B and C in whether the family row is a Product; B differs from C in one nullable flag on one foreign key.
A's invariant surface is a list, not a number: twelve sellable-only consumers plus the two online pointers on Product plus the search key. Each is a one-line rule; the risk is the one that is forgotten.
Promo does not need the family row in any shape. PromoProductLine targets ProductRange, an M2M of products; the family row is only a convenient way to fill a range.
**The Merchant feed keys on product.id, not upc,** so item_group_id is a one-field addition in every shape: A skips parents, B suppresses singletons, C needs neither.
**The search index is the one consumer that reads upc as an identifier** (guid). A must change that; B and C do not.
Replication is the default content policy of B and C, and it is the same policy the marketplaces enforce — and the same policy that produced today's drifting Indomie rows. The tooling to keep members aligned is the real cost, not the table.

#### Reading

Our closed pieces are Amazon's and Magento's structure: a parent row in the same table (shape A) and an axis that is a marked attribute. Both of those platforms keep the type separate from the category. The systems where the type has teeth over variants are all marketplaces or the PIM; the merchant storefront tools (Shopify, Square, WooCommerce) leave the family free.

Decision | Question | Where the thirteen split |
